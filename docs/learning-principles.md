# Learning principles for generated study plans

The plan generator follows these principles. Each one is backed by research
on how children learn and remember; sources are listed at the end. An
example week built from them is in
[example-weekly-plan.md](example-weekly-plan.md).

The three goals are:

- **Knowledge retention:** the child still knows it weeks later, not just
  tomorrow.
- **Self-motivated learning:** the child wants to do the session and feels
  in control of it.
- **Curiosity:** the child leaves with a question they want answered.

## 1. Retention

### Retrieval practice ("Weißt du noch?")
Recalling something from memory strengthens it far more than re-reading it.
Every session starts with a few questions answered **from memory, without
looking**, and the Friday session is a mixed quiz. Low stakes: no grades,
mistakes are expected. [Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Dunlosky et al. 2013]

### Spaced repetition
Practice spread over days and weeks beats the same amount of practice in one
block. A topic comes back at growing intervals. [Cepeda et al. 2006;
Dunlosky et al. 2013]

Default schedule after a topic is first practised (a Leitner-style
heuristic, not an exact optimum):

| Step | Next review |
|------|-------------|
| 1 | next day |
| 2 | 2–3 days later (same week) |
| 3 | next week |
| 4 | 2 weeks later |
| 5 | 4 weeks later |

Ratings adjust it: **hard** (by parent or child) moves the topic back to
step 1, **ok** advances one step, **easy** advances two steps.

### Interleaving
Mixing different kinds of problems within a session (e.g. 2-, 5- and
10-times tables together, or maths and spelling items in the warm-up) helps
children learn *which* strategy fits, instead of repeating the last one.
Introduce a topic on its own first, then mix it in. [Rohrer & Taylor 2007;
Dunlosky et al. 2013]

### Generation and self-explanation ("Erklär es mir")
Children remember more of what they produce themselves (filling a gap,
finding examples, drawing, explaining) than what they only read. Each
session ends the main part with the child **explaining** something to the
parent in their own words. [Slamecka & Graf 1978; Fiorella & Mayer 2016;
Dunlosky et al. 2013 (self-explanation: moderate utility)]

### Words plus pictures
Combining a short text with a simple picture, diagram or real objects
(coins, socks, fingers) helps, especially at age 6–10. Use concrete objects
first, then numbers. [Mayer 2009 (multimedia/dual coding)]

## 2. Self-motivated learning

### Autonomy, competence, relatedness
Motivation that lasts comes from feeling in control (autonomy), feeling
able to succeed (competence) and doing it with someone who cares
(relatedness). [Ryan & Deci 2000, self-determination theory]

What the plan does with this:
- **Choice:** most days offer two versions of the main task (e.g. pictures
  or numbers) and the child picks. Small, meaningful choices increase
  intrinsic motivation. [Patall, Cooper & Robinson 2008]
- **Right difficulty:** tasks should be solvable with some effort. Ratings
  push the next plan easier or harder.
- **Done together:** the session is short, at a fixed time, with the parent
  nearby and interested.

### Feedback and praise
Feedback about the task and the strategy ("you checked by counting the
pairs, good idea") helps; praise for being clever can make children avoid
hard tasks after failure. [Mueller & Dweck 1998; Hattie & Timperley 2007]

### No tangible rewards for the session
Expected tangible rewards (sweets, money, screen time) for doing an
activity tend to reduce children's own interest in it. Use informational,
specific feedback instead. [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999; Lepper, Greene &
Nisbett 1973]

### Self-assessment ("Ampel")
Before checking answers, the child rates their own confidence (green,
yellow, red). This trains self-monitoring, a core part of self-regulated
learning, and gives the app the child's rating. [EEF 2021, Metacognition
and self-regulated learning]

## 3. Curiosity

### Information gaps ("Rätsel des Tages")
Curiosity arises when a child notices a gap between what they know and what
they want to know. A short puzzle or surprising question before the
content opens that gap. [Loewenstein 1994]

### Predict first
Guessing an answer before learning it, even wrongly, improves later memory
of the correct answer. The puzzle asks the child to **guess first**.
[Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009]

### Curiosity helps memory
When children are curious about a question, they remember the answer
better, and even unrelated information learned in that state.
[Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014]

### Child's own questions ("Frage für morgen", "Forscherfrage")
Each session ends with the child saying one thing they wonder about. On
Friday the child picks one of the week's questions to find out together.
This gives the child ownership and shows that questions are valued.

## Session template (20 minutes)

Every day uses the same shape, so the routine itself becomes familiar.
Minutes scale down if the cap is lowered.

| Block | Minutes | Principle |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| Weißt du noch? (3 recall questions, from memory) | 3 | Retrieval, spacing, interleaving |
| Rätsel des Tages (guess first) | 2 | Curiosity, prediction |
| Hauptaufgabe (main task, often with a choice) | 10 | Generation, concrete examples, autonomy |
| Erklär es mir (child explains) | 3 | Self-explanation |
| Ampel + Frage für morgen | 2 | Self-assessment, curiosity |

Friday replaces the main task with a mixed quiz and the child's chosen
"Forscherfrage".

## What we deliberately leave out

- **Learning styles** (visual/auditory learners): matching teaching to a
  "style" has no good evidence. [Pashler et al. 2008]
- **Growth-mindset slogans:** stand-alone mindset interventions show small
  average effects. We use the well-supported part (feedback on effort and
  strategy) without lectures. [Sisk et al. 2018]
- **Long sessions and cramming:** short, daily, spaced sessions instead.
